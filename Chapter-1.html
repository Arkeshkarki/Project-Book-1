Computer System

Introduction of Computer

A computer is an electronic device that accepts raw data and instructions from users, processes it according to a given set of instructions, stores the processed data, and produces meaningful information as output. The word "Computer" is derived from the Latin word "Computare," which means 'to calculate'. Modern computers are versatile machines capable of performing complex mathematical calculations accurately and quickly, as well as handling tasks like creating documents, videos, games, and presentations.

Key Characteristics of a Computer

Speed: Computers can perform calculations and process information at incredibly high speeds, often measured in millions of instructions per second (MIPS). Accuracy: Computers are highly accurate and perform tasks without errors, provided the input data and instructions are correct. Diligence: Unlike humans, computers do not suffer from boredom, fatigue, or lack of concentration. They can perform repetitive tasks with the same speed and accuracy. Versatility: A computer can perform a wide range of tasks, from complex scientific calculations to simple word processing, making it a truly multipurpose device. Storage Capacity: Computers have the ability to store vast amounts of data and information, both temporarily (primary memory) and permanently (secondary memory). Reliability: Computers are generally reliable and consistent in their performance.

Evolution of Computer Technology (Generations)

The evolution of computers is categorized into generations, each marked by significant technological advancements that led to smaller, faster, cheaper, and more efficient machines.

1. First Generation (1940s-1950s): Vacuum Tubes

Technology: Used vacuum tubes for circuitry and magnetic drums for memory. Characteristics: Very large in size, consumed enormous amounts of electricity, generated a lot of heat, and were expensive. Programming was done in machine language. Examples: ENIAC, UNIVAC.

2. Second Generation (1950s-1960s): Transistors

Technology: Replaced vacuum tubes with transistors. Characteristics: Smaller, faster, more reliable, and consumed less power than first-generation computers. High-level programming languages like FORTRAN and COBOL were introduced. Examples: IBM 7000 series, CDC 1604.

3. Third Generation (1960s-1970s): Integrated Circuits (ICs)

Technology: Used Integrated Circuits (ICs), which combined multiple transistors on a single silicon chip. Characteristics: Further reduction in size and cost, increased speed and efficiency. Operating systems were introduced, allowing computers to run multiple applications simultaneously. Examples: IBM System/360, PDP-8.

4. Fourth Generation (1970s-Present): Microprocessors

Technology: Utilized Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) technology, leading to the development of microprocessors. A microprocessor contains all the components of a computer's central processing unit (CPU) on a single chip. Characteristics: Personal computers (PCs) became widely available. Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs), mouse, and handheld devices emerged. Networking and the internet began to develop. Examples: Apple Macintosh, IBM PC.

5. Fifth Generation (Present and Beyond): Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Technology: Based on Artificial Intelligence (AI) and parallel processing. Characteristics: Aim to develop computers that can understand natural language, learn, reason, and make decisions. Focus on areas like robotics, expert systems, and natural language processing. Examples: AI-powered systems, quantum computing, neural networks. Measurement Unit of Processing Speed and Storage Unit Understanding how computer speed and storage are measured is essential for evaluating performance.

Processing Speed:

The processing speed of a computer, particularly its CPU, is measured in cycles per second, known as Hertz (Hz). Hertz (Hz): One cycle per second. Kilohertz (KHz): 1,000 Hz Megahertz (MHz): 1,000 KHz or 1,000,000 Hz Gigahertz (GHz): 1,000 MHz or 1,000,000,000 Hz (Most modern CPUs operate in the GHz range). A higher clock speed generally indicates faster processing, though other factors like core count and architecture also play a significant role. Storage Unit: Computer storage is measured in bits and bytes, which represent binary digits (0s and 1s). Bit (b): The smallest unit of data, a binary digit (0 or 1). Nibble: 4 bits. Byte (B): 8 bits. This is the fundamental unit for storing a single character (e.g., 'A', '1', '$'). Kilobyte (KB): 1,024 bytes. Megabyte (MB): 1,024 KB. Gigabyte (GB): 1,024 MB. Terabyte (TB): 1,024 GB. Petabyte (PB): 1,024 TB. These units help quantify the capacity of memory and storage devices like RAM, hard drives, and pen drives. Types of Computers (Super, Mainframe, Mini, Microcomputers) Computers can be classified based on their size, speed, cost, and capabilities. 1. Supercomputers: Characteristics: The fastest, most powerful, and most expensive computers. They can perform trillions of calculations per second. Applications: Used for highly complex scientific and engineering problems, weather forecasting, climate research, nuclear research, and advanced simulations. Example: Summit (IBM), Fugaku (Fujitsu). 2. Mainframe Computers: Characteristics: Large, powerful, and expensive computers capable of supporting hundreds or thousands of users simultaneously. They have high processing power and large storage capacity. Applications: Used by large organizations for critical applications like bulk data processing, enterprise resource planning (ERP), transaction processing (e.g., banking, airlines), and scientific research. Example: IBM zSeries. 3. Minicomputers (Midrange Computers): Characteristics: Smaller and less powerful than mainframes but more powerful than microcomputers. They can support dozens to hundreds of users. Applications: Used by small to medium-sized organizations for specific tasks like process control, data management, and scientific instrumentation. Example: DEC PDP series, IBM AS/400. 4. Microcomputers (Personal Computers - PCs): Characteristics: Smallest, least expensive, and most common type of computer. Designed for individual users. Applications: Widely used in homes, schools, offices, and small businesses for tasks like word processing, internet browsing, gaming, and multimedia. Types: Desktops, Laptops, Tablets, Smartphones, Workstations. Example: Desktop PCs, Apple MacBooks, smartphones. In Nepal, microcomputers are ubiquitous in educational institutions, government offices, and personal use. Mobile Computing and its Applications Mobile computing refers to the use of portable computing devices and wireless networks to access information and applications from virtually anywhere. It has revolutionized how we interact with technology and the world. Key Concepts: Portability: Devices are small and lightweight, allowing users to carry them easily. Connectivity: Relies on wireless networks (Wi-Fi, cellular data like 4G/5G) for internet access. Interactivity: Often features touchscreens, voice commands, and sensors for user interaction. Applications of Mobile Computing: Communication: Instant messaging, video calls, email, social media (e.g., Viber, WhatsApp, Facebook widely used in Nepal). Education: E-learning platforms, educational apps, online research, accessing digital notes like these. Business: Mobile banking (e.g., eSewa, Khalti in Nepal), online shopping, mobile payments, remote work, sales force automation. Navigation: GPS services, mapping applications (e.g., Google Maps). Entertainment: Mobile gaming, streaming music and videos, e-books. Healthcare: Fitness trackers, health monitoring apps, telemedicine. Emergency Services: Quick access to emergency contacts, location sharing. Concept of Computer Architecture and Organization Computer Architecture: This refers to the conceptual design and fundamental operational structure of a computer system. It describes what the computer does and what it needs to do. It includes the instruction set architecture, memory organization, and input/output mechanisms. It's like the blueprint of a building. Computer Organization: This refers to the physical implementation of the computer architecture. It describes how the computer system is structured and how its components are interconnected to implement the architecture. It deals with the operational units and their interconnections that realize the architectural specifications. It's like the actual construction of the building based on the blueprint. In essence, architecture focuses on the user's perspective (what is visible to the programmer), while organization focuses on the hardware's perspective (how the components are connected and function). Components of Computer System (Input unit, Output unit, CPU, Memory unit) A computer system fundamentally operates on the Input-Process-Output (IPO) cycle. The main components that facilitate this cycle are: 1. Input Unit: Function: Accepts data and instructions from the user and converts them into a form that the computer can understand (binary code). Examples: Keyboard, Mouse, Scanner, Microphone. 2. Central Processing Unit (CPU): Function: Often called the "brain" of the computer, the CPU performs all arithmetic and logical operations, controls the flow of data, and executes instructions. Components of CPU: Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU): Performs arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) and logical operations (comparisons like greater than, less than, equal to). Control Unit (CU): Manages and coordinates all the components of the computer system. It fetches instructions from memory, decodes them, and directs the other units to perform the necessary operations. Registers: Small, high-speed storage locations within the CPU used to temporarily hold data and instructions during processing. 3. Memory Unit: Function: Stores data and instructions. It is categorized into primary and secondary memory. Primary Memory (Main Memory): Directly accessible by the CPU. It holds data and instructions that the CPU is currently working on. (e.g., RAM, ROM). Secondary Memory (Auxiliary Memory): Provides long-term storage for data and programs that are not currently in use. (e.g., Hard Disk, SSD, Pen Drive). 4. Output Unit: Function: Converts the processed information (results) from the computer's binary form into a human-readable format. Examples: Monitor, Printer, Speaker. Microprocessor (Basic Concepts, Clock Speed, Word Length) A microprocessor is a computer processor that incorporates the functions of a central processing unit (CPU) on a single integrated circuit (IC), or at most a few integrated circuits. It is the core component of a computer that performs most of the processing. Basic Concepts: It contains the ALU, CU, and registers. It fetches instructions, decodes them, executes them, and stores the results. Clock Speed: Measured in Hertz (Hz), it indicates how many instruction cycles the microprocessor can execute per second. A 2 GHz processor can execute 2 billion cycles per second. Higher clock speed generally means faster processing. Word Length: Refers to the number of bits that the CPU can process at one time. Common word lengths are 32-bit and 64-bit. A 64-bit processor can process more data simultaneously than a 32-bit processor, leading to faster performance and the ability to access more memory. Primary Memory (RAM, ROM, types) Primary memory, also known as main memory, is directly accessible by the CPU and is crucial for the computer's operation. 1. RAM (Random Access Memory): Nature: Volatile memory, meaning it loses its contents when the power is turned off. Function: Used to temporarily store data and programs that the CPU is currently using. Data can be read from and written to RAM quickly. Types: SRAM (Static RAM): Faster and more expensive than DRAM. Used for cache memory. It retains data as long as power is supplied and does not need to be refreshed. DRAM (Dynamic RAM): Slower and less expensive than SRAM. Used for the main system memory. It needs to be constantly refreshed to retain data. 2. ROM (Read Only Memory): Nature: Non-volatile memory, meaning its contents are retained even when the power is turned off. Function: Stores permanent instructions (firmware) required to start up the computer (BIOS - Basic Input/Output System). Data can typically only be read from ROM, not written to it by the user. Types: PROM (Programmable ROM): Can be programmed once by the user. EPROM (Erasable Programmable ROM): Can be erased by exposure to ultraviolet light and then reprogrammed. EEPROM (Electrically Erasable Programmable ROM): Can be erased and reprogrammed electrically, without removing it from the circuit. Secondary Memory (Magnetic Disk, Flash Memory, CD, DVD, SSD, Pen Drive) Secondary memory, also known as auxiliary storage, provides long-term, non-volatile storage for data and programs. It is not directly accessible by the CPU and is slower than primary memory but offers much larger storage capacity at a lower cost. 1. Magnetic Disk (Hard Disk Drive - HDD): Description: Uses rapidly rotating platters coated with magnetic material to store data. Data is read and written by magnetic heads. Characteristics: High storage capacity (terabytes), relatively inexpensive per gigabyte, but slower and more susceptible to physical damage than SSDs. 2. Flash Memory: Description: A non-volatile computer memory that can be electrically erased and reprogrammed. Characteristics: Durable, fast, and consumes less power. Used in various devices. 3. CD (Compact Disc): Description: An optical storage medium. Data is stored and read using a laser. Characteristics: Typically stores up to 700 MB of data. Used for music, software distribution, and data backup. 4. DVD (Digital Versatile Disc): Description: An optical storage medium similar to a CD but with higher storage capacity. Characteristics: Stores 4.7 GB (single-layer) to 8.5 GB (dual-layer) of data. Used for movies, high-capacity software, and data backup. 5. SSD (Solid State Drive): Description: Uses flash memory to store data, similar to a large USB flash drive. Characteristics: Much faster than HDDs (due to no moving parts), more durable, consumes less power, but generally more expensive per gigabyte. Increasingly common in modern laptops and high-performance desktops. 6. Pen Drive (USB Flash Drive): Description: A portable flash memory data storage device integrated with a USB interface. Characteristics: Small, lightweight, durable, and convenient for transferring data between computers. Capacities range from a few gigabytes to several terabytes. Input Devices (Keyboard, Mouse, Scanner, Light Pen, OMR, Microphone, Webcam) Input devices allow users to feed data and instructions into a computer system. Keyboard: The most common input device for entering text, numbers, and commands. Mouse: A pointing device used to control a cursor on the screen, select items, and interact with graphical user interfaces. Scanner: Converts physical documents or images into digital format. Light Pen: A pointing device used to select objects on a display screen by touching it to the screen. OMR (Optical Mark Reader): Reads marks made by pencil or pen on a document, commonly used for processing multiple-choice answer sheets in exams (e.g., entrance exams in Nepal). Microphone: Captures sound and converts it into digital audio, used for voice input, recording, and communication. Webcam: A digital camera connected to a computer, used for video conferencing, live streaming, and capturing images/videos. Output Devices (Monitor, Printer, Speaker, Headphones, Projector) Output devices display or present the processed information from the computer in a human-understandable format. Monitor: The primary visual output device, displaying text, images, and video. Types include CRT, LCD, LED, and OLED. Printer: Produces hard copies of digital documents on paper. Types include inkjet (for color and photos) and laser (for high-speed text). Speaker: Produces audio output, allowing users to hear sounds, music, and voice. Headphones: Personal audio output devices worn over or in the ears, providing private listening. Projector: Displays computer output onto a large screen or wall, commonly used for presentations in classrooms and meeting rooms. Hardware Interfaces Hardware interfaces are the points of connection between a computer and its peripheral devices, or between different internal components. They define how data is exchanged. USB (Universal Serial Bus): A standard interface for connecting a wide range of peripheral devices (keyboards, mice, printers, flash drives, smartphones) to a computer. It supports hot-swapping (connecting/disconnecting devices while the computer is on). HDMI (High-Definition Multimedia Interface): A digital interface for transmitting high-definition video and audio from a source device (e.g., computer, DVD player) to a display device (e.g., monitor, TV, projector). VGA (Video Graphics Array): An older analog interface for connecting a computer to a monitor or projector. It transmits only video signals. Ethernet Port (RJ-45): Used for wired network connections, allowing computers to connect to a local area network (LAN) or the internet. Audio Jacks (3.5mm): Standard ports for connecting headphones, speakers, and microphones. Power Connector: Connects the computer to a power supply. SATA (Serial Advanced Technology Attachment): An interface used to connect storage devices like hard disk drives (HDDs) and solid-state drives (SSDs) to the computer's motherboard. PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express): A high-speed expansion bus interface used to connect high-performance components like graphics cards, network cards, and SSDs to the motherboard.
